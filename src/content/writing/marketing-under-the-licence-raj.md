For forty-four years India ran an economy in which demand reliably exceeded
supply, and it did so on purpose. Between the Industries (Development and
Regulation) Act of 1951 and the delicensing of July 1991, a firm could not build
a plant, expand a line, diversify its products or move its location without a
licence from the central government. Capacity was not a commercial judgement. It
was an application.

What that regime did to consumers has been answered many times over. The
question I want to ask is narrower and, I think, more revealing: what did it do
to *marketing*? If output is capped by statute and buyers are already queuing,
then persuasion, differentiation and positioning have nothing to do. So what
were the people nominally responsible for them doing all day?

The answer is not that marketing disappeared. It is that marketing **changed
job**. Under the Licence Raj it stopped being the function that created demand
and became the function that rationed supply. And the firms that walked out of
1991 alive were, with some consistency, the ones where that substitution never
fully took hold.

## The machinery of guaranteed demand

Three statutes did most of the work. The IDRA of 1951 made an industrial licence
a precondition for almost any manufacturing activity. The Monopolies and
Restrictive Trade Practices Act of 1969 added a second approval layer for large
firms specifically, on the theory that scale was itself a hazard. The Foreign
Exchange Regulation Act of 1973 capped foreign equity and, in 1977, was applied
strictly enough that Coca-Cola, IBM, Kodak and Mobil left the country rather
than dilute.

The combined effect was a **sellers' market held in place by law**. János
Kornai, writing about the Eastern Bloc rather than India, gave the resulting
condition its name and its theory: in a shortage economy, allocation stops
running through price and starts running through queues, rationing and informal
exchange. Kornai's cases were centrally planned. India's was not — it had
private ownership, private profit and, formally, a market. But on the specific
question of whether a firm could increase output to meet demand, the answer was
the same, and so the behaviour was the same.

The queues are the evidence, and they were extraordinary. A Bajaj Chetak carried
a waiting period reported at up to ten years, and traded in the secondary market
at close to double its showroom price — the scooter appreciated by being owned.
An officially sanctioned route existed to jump it: surrender $500 in foreign
exchange and receive an out-of-turn allotment, which tells you that the
government understood perfectly well that the queue was a currency. A telephone
connection took five to seven years. When Maruti opened bookings in April 1983,
around 1.2 lakh people paid ₹10,000 in advance against a wait of roughly three
years, and the allotment was settled by **lottery**. The first car in independent
India's most successful automotive launch went to a man who won a draw.

Read that last fact as a marketer rather than a historian. The launch event for
the Maruti 800 was not an advertisement. It was a raffle.

## What the function became

Once demand is guaranteed, the scarce resource inside the firm is no longer the
customer. It is the stock. And whoever controls the allocation of stock holds the
commercially decisive job, whatever the org chart calls them.

So the marketing function in a licensed Indian firm did real, difficult,
consequential work — it simply was not the work in the textbooks. It managed
dealer allotments. It decided which distributor got units this quarter and which
waited. It handled the political economy of the waiting list, which is to say it
decided who was permitted to jump it. This is closer to a rationing board than to
a brand team, and it produced a corresponding skill set: excellent at
relationships and allocation, untested at persuasion and price.

The general claim underneath is worth stating plainly, because it is usually
left implicit. **Marketing is not a universal function of enterprise.** It is
what firms do when supply exceeds demand. Reverse that condition and the
discipline does not become harder — it becomes unnecessary, and the department
survives by finding other work. India ran that experiment at national scale for
four decades.

## Where marketing actually survived

The tidy version of this argument would be that Indian marketing simply stopped
between 1951 and 1991. That version is wrong, and the exceptions are the most
useful part of the story.

Licensing bit hardest on capital-intensive, large-scale, formally organised
industry. It barely reached the small-scale reserved sector, the cooperatives, or
categories where the barrier to entry was a room and a supplier. Marketing
persisted in exactly those places — not as a survival, but because the ordinary
condition of a buyers' market persisted there too.

In 1969, Karsanbhai Patel began making detergent in a room in his house in
Ahmedabad and selling it door to door on his bicycle on the way to work. Nirma
was priced around ₹3.50 a kilogram against Surf's ₹13. By 1988 it is reported to
have held roughly 60% of the Indian detergent market and had forced Hindustan
Lever — the most sophisticated marketing organisation in the country — to
respond with Wheel. That is a complete competitive narrative, with pricing,
segmentation, distribution and an incumbent response, conducted entirely inside
the Licence Raj.

Amul is the same lesson from the opposite direction. Its three-tier cooperative
structure — village societies, district unions, state federation — was built to
solve procurement and distribution across tens of thousands of villages, in a
category where the constraint was logistics rather than licensing.

And Thums Up exists only because of the 1977 exits: Parle formulated it directly
into the vacuum Coca-Cola left behind, and by the time Coca-Cola returned it had
to buy the brand rather than beat it, acquiring Thums Up, Limca and Gold Spot in
1993 for a reported $60 million at a point when Thums Up is said to have held
around 85% of the cola market.

The Licence Raj did not abolish marketing. It **relocated** it, to the parts of
the economy the licence did not reach.

## 1991 as a natural experiment

Delicensing in July 1991 removed the requirement for all but eighteen strategic
industries and dismantled the MRTP thresholds. For our purposes it functions as a
natural experiment: it lifted the supply constraint more or less at once, across
most of the economy, and let us observe which firms had been holding an asset and
which had been holding a queue.

| Firm | Position before 1991 | Source of that position | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HMT Watches | c. 90% of the Indian watch market | Licensed capacity, no domestic rival | First loss 1994; watch business closed May 2016 |
| Hindustan Motors | c. 70%+ of passenger cars | Protected production, minimal model change | 2,200 Ambassadors sold in 2014; production ended |
| Nirma | c. 60% of detergent | Price and door-to-door distribution | Survived the entry of global competitors |
| Amul | Dominant in organised dairy | Cooperative procurement and distribution | Survived FDI in dairy; now exports widely |
| Thums Up | c. 85% of cola | Filled a vacuum, then advertised hard | Acquired rather than defeated, 1993 |

The pattern is legible. Firms whose position came from **allocation** did not
survive the end of allocation. HMT had made watches with Citizen from 1961 and
owned the category outright; it met Titan's quartz designs with mechanical
movements and a monopolist's reflexes, lost money for the first time in 1994 and
never made it back. The Ambassador held above 70% of the car market and had, by
2014, sold 2,200 units in a year. Neither firm was destroyed by a superior
competitor so much as by the removal of the thing that had been doing the
selling.

Firms whose position came from **distribution and price** did survive. That is
the finding, and it is not a coincidence: distribution is an asset that has to be
built against resistance, so it constitutes evidence about the firm in a way that
a licence never does.

## The case that complicates it

I want to be careful not to overstate this, because the cleanest counter-example
is the firm with the longest queue.

Bajaj had both. It had the ten-year Chetak waiting list, and it had a genuine
national dealer and service network built over decades. On the argument above it
should have sailed through liberalisation. It did not. Through the 1990s the
geared scooter lost to 100–125cc four-stroke motorcycles on economy and
ergonomics; Bajaj lost two-wheeler leadership to Hero Honda around 2001, the same
year Honda's Activa took the gearless scooter market and Bajaj answered with the
Pulsar. It reinvented itself as a performance motorcycle company, exited scooters
entirely in 2009, and still watched its overall two-wheeler share fall from
around 27% to roughly 12% by FY17.

So distribution was **necessary but not sufficient**. The more accurate statement
is that distribution bought time. It kept Bajaj solvent and present for the
decade it took to learn how to compete on product and brand — a decade HMT and
Hindustan Motors also had and did not use. The asset was not the answer. It was
the runway.

## Why this is not only history

The generalisable claim is this: **a firm operating in a sellers' market is
accumulating an asset it has never tested, and cannot distinguish from one it
has.** Market share under scarcity and market share under competition look
identical on a slide and behave completely differently under stress.

That condition has not gone anywhere; it has only become voluntary. Luxury
houses now manufacture the Licence Raj's exact conditions on purpose — capped
output, allocation by relationship, authorised-dealer waiting lists, a secondary
market above retail. It is treated as the strongest possible evidence of brand
equity. India already ran the experiment on whether a waiting list is equity or
merely a queue, and the answer was that you cannot tell until the constraint
lifts, at which point you find out very quickly and cannot do anything about it.

The same question sits under every allocation-driven category today: compute
capacity sold on relationship rather than price, protected or subsidised
manufacturing under industrial policy, and — closer to home — shelf space on
quick-commerce platforms, where visibility is allocated by an intermediary rather
than earned from a shopper. In each case a firm's position is partly a function
of someone else's constraint.

The test is a single question, and it is worth asking before the constraint
lifts rather than after: **if supply doubled tomorrow, what exactly would still
be ours?** HMT could not answer it in 1991. It had thirty years of dominance and
five years left.

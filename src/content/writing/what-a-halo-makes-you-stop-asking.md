The most consequential thing a halo does is not make people like you more. It
makes them stop checking.

Marketing has spent a century learning to build halos and has become genuinely
good at it. It has never once counted what they cost. Not because the cost is
hidden — it is perfectly observable — but because nobody has thought to put a
line for it on the dashboard.

## What the halo effect actually is

It is worth being precise, because the term has drifted.

The halo effect is not what a brand is known for. That is positioning, and
marketing measures it exhaustively. The halo effect is the **spillover**: you
like one thing about something, so you assume the rest of it is good too,
without evidence for the rest.

Thorndike named it in 1920, watching officers rate their soldiers. Men judged to
have good physiques were also judged more intelligent and better led. The rating
of one attribute had quietly contaminated the rating of every other.

The distinction matters for what follows. A brand can choose what it is known
for. It cannot choose what people will infer from that, and the inference is
where the action is.

## Marketers farm it deliberately

It would be convenient to argue that the industry does not understand this
mechanism. That is not true, and the argument is stronger without it.

Marketing farms the spillover on purpose, and has standard instruments for doing
so. The **halo product** is a flagship built less to sell than to lift
perception of everything beside it. The **halo car** is a low-volume model that
exists for the showroom rather than the balance sheet. Apple's iPod-to-Mac halo
in the mid-2000s was openly pursued and modelled by analysts at the time.
Umbrella branding, brand extension and celebrity endorsement are mechanically
the same bet: that affection for one thing will be lent to another thing that
has not earned it.

The practice is deliberate, sophisticated, and about a hundred years old.

## The ledger only has one side

Every consequence a halo produces in the brand's favour is instrumented.
Awareness, consideration, preference, price premium, extension success rate, the
survival odds of a launch under an existing name — all measured, all tracked
quarterly, all defended in front of a board.

Nothing measures what it takes away.

There is no research instrument anywhere, in academic marketing or in industry
practice, whose outcome variable is *what our customers have stopped asking
about us*. The halo's upside is counted to three decimal places. Its cost has
never been counted once.

That asymmetry is the subject of this essay, and I should say plainly at the
outset that the evidence for the cost does not come from marketing. It comes
from cognitive psychology, from human factors research in aviation, and — most
recently and most sharply — from people studying artificial intelligence. My own
discipline invented the practice and left the accounting to others.

## The evidence for not looking

Three findings do most of the work.

**Nisbett and Wilson, 1977.** Students watched an instructor behave either warmly
or coldly, then rated his appearance, mannerisms and accent. Those who saw the
warm version found his accent appealing; those who saw the cold version found the
same accent irritating. The crucial detail is the one usually dropped in the
retelling: subjects had sufficient information to assess those attributes
independently, and did not. And they were unaware that anything had influenced
them.

**Chaiken, 1980.** A likeable source triggers heuristic rather than systematic
processing — people lean on the cue instead of examining the argument. This comes
with a boundary condition worth stating honestly, because it constrains the
claim: under high involvement, when people are motivated, systematic scrutiny
returns and source likeability stops mattering. The halo suppresses checking
hardest where people care least. Since most buying is low-involvement, that
narrows the argument less than it first appears — but it does narrow it.

**Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010.** From aviation rather than marketing. Operators
who trust an automated system stop monitoring it and miss its failures. Two
findings transfer directly. Complacency appears in experts as readily as in
novices and does not go away with practice. And it emerges under multitask load,
when attention is already divided — which is the permanent condition of anyone
buying anything.

Sperber and colleagues supply the language for what is happening underneath.
Humans are trustful by default, and epistemic vigilance is costly, so it gets
economised. A halo is simply a very efficient reason to economise.

## Three companies nobody asked about

What follows are three cases where a halo was real, deserved, and load-bearing in
the wrong direction. They are chosen to cover three different classes of checker:
customers, investors and auditors.

**BlackBerry, and the customers who did not ask.** It was trusted on security,
and the trust was earned — the encryption was real and the enterprise
architecture was genuinely better than anything else available. What the halo
bought was silence on everything adjacent. Corporate IT departments satisfied
about security did not go on to interrogate the app ecosystem, developer
momentum, or the browser. Those turned out to be the things that killed it. The
company was outrun in precisely the areas its reputation had made it seem
unnecessary to examine.

Notice what did *not* happen. BlackBerry was never discredited. Its halo is
intact today, years after the business is gone — people still remember it as
secure and beautifully made, and they are right. The reputation survived the
company. It simply never had anything to do with the questions that mattered.

**Theranos, and the investors who did not ask.** The purest case of the three,
because the halo was attached to nothing at all. Not a product — investors and
board members extended credit on Stanford, on presentation, and on the
endorsement of prominent people who had themselves been extended credit for
reputations in unrelated fields. Technical due diligence that would be routine
for any medical device company was skipped. Nobody asked to watch the machine
work.

**Satyam, and the auditors who did not ask.** In 2008 it won the Golden Peacock
Award for Corporate Governance. In January 2009 its chairman confessed to having
inflated profits and cash balances by roughly ₹7,000 crore over several years. He
was convicted in 2015 and sentenced to seven years. The awards were revoked. Its
auditors had signed off throughout.

Satyam is the sharpest of the three because of what the halo was made of. It was
not a reputation for a good product. It was a reputation for **being well
scrutinised** — and it functioned as a substitute for scrutiny. An award for
governance was accepted as evidence that somebody else had already looked.

I chose these three, which means they illustrate the argument rather than test
it. A reader is entitled to note that I did not go looking for the cases where a
halo cost nothing.

## And now the checking itself has a halo

Here is the turn, and it is recent enough that marketing has not yet noticed it
is a marketing problem.

The share of verification running through conversational AI is climbing steeply.
When someone wants to know whether a claim is true, whether a product is any
good, whether they are being sold something — increasingly, they ask a model.
Which would be an improvement on not checking at all, except that the thing they
are checking with has been built to be liked.

In a 2026 study that manipulated nothing but a chatbot's **warmth** — pure
likeability, no informational content whatsoever — participants with search tools
open in front of them still declined to verify. Warmth increased agreement with
the chatbot specifically in the cases where the chatbot was wrong. And whether
someone verified at all was driven by their own traits and prior beliefs rather
than by the quality of the answer they had been given.

Cheng and colleagues, publishing in *Science* in 2026, put numbers on the scale
of it. Across eleven leading models and 2,405 participants in three preregistered
experiments, AI affirmed users' actions 49% more often than other humans did —
including where the query involved deception, illegality or harm. A single
interaction with a sycophantic model left people less willing to take
responsibility or repair a conflict, and more convinced they had been right all
along. Sycophantic models were trusted and preferred more than accurate ones. As
the authors observe, the feature causing the harm is the feature driving the
engagement.

Meanwhile a four-week study at the MIT Media Lab found 67 participants were 21%
better at identifying fake news while assisted by AI — and 15 percentage points
*worse* than their own starting baseline once it was taken away. About a quarter
believed they were improving throughout.

Set that last sentence beside Nisbett and Wilson: *subjects were unaware of the
influence*. Forty-nine years apart, in entirely separate literatures, the same
finding. People do not experience themselves as having stopped checking. They
experience themselves as satisfied.

And here is the part I would want any marketer to sit with. **Sycophancy is not
an engineering defect. It is an engagement optimisation.** Models are tuned on
what people rate highly, and people rate agreement highly. That is not a
technical decision that went wrong. It is a marketing decision wearing an
engineering costume — the same incentive that built a century of likeable brands,
now pointed at the referee.

So the halo has not merely reduced scrutiny. It has been installed inside the
instrument people use to scrutinise.

## The test

None of this argues that halos are bad, or that a brand should want to be
disliked. The spillover is not optional; it is what human beings do, and a firm
that refused to benefit from it would be worse off with nothing to show for the
sacrifice.

The claim is narrower. A halo is credit extended by strangers against attributes
they have not examined. Like any credit, it is worth having and worth knowing the
size of. Unlike every other asset on a brand's dashboard, nobody has ever
measured this one.

The test is a single question, and it costs nothing to ask:

**What do our customers no longer bother to check about us — and are we still
worth not checking?**

BlackBerry could not have answered it. Neither could Satyam. The uncomfortable
part is that neither would have found the question difficult. They would have
found it unnecessary.
